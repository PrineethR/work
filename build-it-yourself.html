Build-It-Yourself

Thakkarbapanagar English School, Ahmedabad
2019 — 2020

Role Concept, research, design
Users Children aged 8–12
Testing 8 teacher–student pairs

Project

Making and DIY culture has arrived in education, and it mostly arrives in a box you have to buy. BIY removes the kit. It is a phygital platform that generates building instructions from the materials a child already has — at home or in the classroom — using AR on the phones most families already own. The starting observation was mundane and local: .

The design problem is a matching problem. If the system is going to adapt to whatever is on hand, materials have to be categorised by what they can do — strength, flexibility, whether they can be cut, folded, joined, coloured — rather than by what they are. Most of the early work was building frameworks for that categorisation and testing whether they survived contact with a real pile of rubbish. Contextual inquiry with 15 children and 6 teachers confirmed that children were most engaged when using familiar objects in unfamiliar ways — the "wait, you can make that from this?" moment.

Four principles governed the design: resource adaptability, so the system offers alternatives when something is missing; phygital integration that does not dilute the hands-on part; learning-centred interaction that prioritises discovery over instruction-following; and a dual-user experience — simple enough for a child of eight, deep enough for the teacher standing behind them. Each one posed its own computational problem, which is a polite way of saying each one was harder to implement than to write down.

From there: flows, screens, and the awkward question of how much to put in front of a ten-year-old at once. The emotional journey we mapped went from excitement at the start, to frustration on encountering missing materials, to either abandonment or pride depending on whether a substitution was found. The interface had to catch people at the frustration point — which is a narrow window when you are eight.

We began with the least digital experiment available — . That told us what guidance is actually valuable mid-build, where frustration shows up, and how children respond to being asked to repurpose things. Two forms got explored, a T-Rex and a helicopter; by popular demand the T-Rex won. Testing with eight teacher–student pairs showed we needed visual confirmation of material substitutions and a much simpler categorisation scheme than the one I was attached to.

The revised architecture covers exploring materials, troubleshooting a build that will not stand up, working offline, and a parent-permission and privacy flow — because a system that learns what a child makes is collecting data about a child, which is a sentence that should make any designer pause.

The moment that settled it: a student explaining, proudly, how she had used a cardboard box from outside her house instead of chart paper. Her pride was not diminished by the substitution — it was larger, because she had solved it herself. What began as designing for resource-limited environments became the strength of the thing. The unsolved part is still the algorithm: suggesting viable alternatives without overwhelming a young user. I suspect it needs more technically strong eyes than mine.
