Naptick

&things — independent consultant
December 2024 — present

Role UX & industrial design
Duration 4-month intensive
Status Ongoing · under NDA
Principles Personal · Poetic

Project

A sleep enhancement device that combines light therapy, ambient sound and voice in a form meant to sit on a modern bedside table. The brief is to serve someone across a whole sleep cycle — evening wind-down, middle-of-night disruption, morning wake-up. My role covers user research, industrial design, physical interaction and the digital experience. This is ongoing work under NDA, so most visuals are omitted.

The central problem is designing for reduced cognitive function. The controls have to be intuitive to someone who is half asleep, while the object still has to be worth looking at when it is doing nothing at all. Contextual inquiry into nightstand setups and evening routines confirmed the obvious thing that is easy to design past: people operate these devices in states of consciousness where reading a label is not on the table. Competitive analysis found most products excel at either light or sound, rarely both, and few have physical controls worth using.

Over thirty narrowed to four directions — stacked forms, tapered cones, faceted geometry, and organic curved profiles referencing the brand's own curve. Scaled 3D prints and foam models tested proportion and physical presence before anything functional got built. The tapered cylinder won because it projects light well and does not fall over.

Light is choreographed rather than switched. Evening sequences shift colour temperature from energising to calming; night illumination gives just enough visibility without suppressing melatonin; morning follows a sunrise progression matched to a sound wake-up that increases with it. Material transitions double as grip points, so the object tells your hand where to hold it in the dark.

Across physical and digital, we drew a hard line about what lives where: time-sensitive and frequent interactions belong to the product, complex scheduling and personalisation to the app, convenience and accessibility to voice. The app has three sections and no more — sleep setup, wake up, settings.

What this project keeps teaching me is that the tactile quality of a physical interface is not only functional. For someone moving between wakefulness and sleep it acts as an anchor, and the transitions — in the light, in the sound, in the interface — are where the whole experience is either gentle or not.
